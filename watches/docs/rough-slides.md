# What Every Nerd Should Know About Watches

---

## Slide 1: Before the Watch — Sundials, Water, Fire, and Sand

- **Sundial** — oldest known timekeeper, dating to ~1500 BCE Egypt. Accurate to only about **±15–30 minutes**, because it needs direct sun and because the sun's own pace varies through the year (the "equation of time"). Useless at night or on a cloudy day.
- **Water clock (clepsydra)** — tracked time by a steady drip from one vessel to another. Invented independently by the Babylonians and Egyptians; worked at night, which sundials couldn't. It was arguably the most accurate timekeeper in the world for close to 2,000 years — but "most accurate" still meant drifting by many minutes a day.
- **Candle clocks and hourglasses** — measured elapsed _intervals_, not time of day. Their accuracy depended entirely on consistent materials: an evenly-made candle, evenly-ground sand. Good for "how long has it been," bad for "what time is it."
- **The common thread:** every one of these tools measured a _continuous process_ (a shadow moving, water dripping, sand falling). None of them ticked. That's about to matter.

## Slide 2: The Escapement — Turning a Fall Into a Tick

- Late 13th century, Europe: the first fully mechanical clocks appear, driven by a **falling weight** on a rope or chain wound around an axle.
- Problem: gravity accelerates a falling weight — it doesn't unwind at a steady rate. Something has to meter it out.
- The answer was the **escapement**: a mechanism (originally the verge-and-foliot) that lets the gear train advance one tooth at a time, at regular intervals, converting a continuous force into discrete, evenly-spaced _ticks_.
- This is the real breakthrough — not just "spring vs. weight" but the shift from measuring a continuous flow (water, sand, sunlight) to counting **regular oscillations**. Every mechanical watch since, and even the quartz watch in your drawer, is a descendant of this idea.
- These early clocks were still rough — they'd drift 15–30 minutes a day — but for the first time, timekeeping didn't depend on the sun, water, or fire.

## Slide 3: Early 1500s — The Mainspring Cuts the Cord

- Around 1500x10, German locksmith **Peter Henlein** of Nuremberg began building small clocks driven by a coiled spring instead of a hanging weight.
- **How it works:** a long, thin ribbon of spring steel is coiled inside a cylindrical barrel. One end hooks to a central arbor, the other to the barrel wall. Winding the arbor twists the spring tighter, storing energy as elastic strain; released, it slowly uncoils and drags the barrel around, driving the gear train.
- The payoff: a clock no longer needed to hang from a wall or ceiling to let its weight fall. It could be small, self-contained, and carried. Henlein's creations — nicknamed "Nuremberg eggs" — were the first portable timepieces.
- The catch: a mainspring's torque isn't constant. It pushes hardest fully wound and weakest near the end of its run, which hurt accuracy — a problem watchmakers spent the next two centuries chipping away at (the **fusee** was an early fix).

---

## Slide 4: 1675 — Huygens and the Hairspring

- Dutch scientist **Christiaan Huygens** attached a coiled **spiral hairspring** to a watch's balance wheel.
- **How it works:** the hairspring acts as a restoring force. As the balance wheel rotates one way, the spring winds up and pulls it back; it swings past center, the spring resists the other direction, and pulls it back again. This turns the balance wheel into a **harmonic oscillator** — like a pendulum, but one that works in any orientation and any position, which a hanging pendulum can't do.
- Before this, portable watches were essentially unregulated and could be off by hours a day. With the hairspring, watch accuracy improved to roughly **±10 minutes a day** — an order of magnitude better, and finally good enough to be genuinely useful.
- (For context: Huygens' _earlier_ invention, the pendulum clock of 1656, had already taken _stationary_ clocks from ~15 minutes of error per day down to about 15 seconds per day. The hairspring did for portable watches what the pendulum had done for clocks on a wall.)

## Slide 5: 1714 — The Longitude Prize

- Sailors could find their **latitude** easily from the stars, but **longitude** at sea was essentially unsolvable — and it was getting ships wrecked.
- The British Parliament's **Longitude Act** offered up to **£20,000** (several million pounds today) to anyone who could determine longitude at sea to within 30 nautical miles after a transatlantic voyage.
- The core idea: if you know the exact time back at your home port _and_ the local time where you are now (from the sun), the difference tells you how far east or west you've traveled. All you need is a clock that can survive a rolling ship, salt air, and huge swings in temperature and humidity without losing time.
- **John Harrison** spent roughly 40 years chasing this, building a series of marine timekeepers (H1 through H4). His **H4** (1759) — a chronometer the size of a large pocket watch — kept time to about a second a month at sea.
- What it produced: the **marine chronometer** as a category, plus real innovations in temperature-compensated bimetallic balances, the grasshopper escapement, and the idea that a small, precise watch could outperform a room-sized astronomical instrument. It set the bar for precision watchmaking for the next 150 years.

## Slide 6: 1850 — Waltham and the Birth of Mass Production

- **Aaron Dennison**, along with Edward Howard and David Davis, founded what would become the **Waltham Watch Company** in Massachusetts.
- Their innovation wasn't a new mechanism — it was borrowing the **"American System" of manufacturing** from the firearms industry: machine-made, genuinely interchangeable parts, built to strict tolerances, instead of watchmakers hand-fitting every component by eye.
- The result: watches that were not only cheaper to make, but **more consistently accurate**, because factory tolerances beat individual craftsmanship at scale.
- This is the moment watches stop being purely bespoke luxury objects. By 1880, the American watch industry that Waltham kicked off was producing millions of watches a year.

## Slide 7: Pocket to Wrist

- For roughly 300 years, the **pocket watch** was the default. Watches worn on the wrist — "wristlets" — existed but were seen as a women's fashion accessory; men who wore one risked looking effeminate.
- The shift started small: during the **Boer War (1899–1902)**, soldiers soldered wire lugs onto their pocket watches and strapped them to their wrists so they could check the time without taking a hand off a weapon.
- **World War I** made it universal. In the trenches, fumbling for a pocket watch while under fire was a real liability. "Trench watches" — with wire lugs, luminous dials, and protective crystal cages — became standard issue for officers and signallers.
- Soldiers came home wearing them, and public opinion flipped fast: by **1930**, wristwatches were outselling pocket watches. It took a war, not a fashion trend, to move the watch from the pocket to the wrist.

## Slide 8: The Modern Men's Wristwatch, by Type

- **Dress watch** — thin, restrained, minimal complications, leather strap. Designed to disappear under a cuff, not draw attention.
- **Tool watches** — purpose-built for a job:
  - **Pilot watch** — oversized, high-legibility dial; large crown, sized to operate with gloves on.
  - **Dive watch** — rotating timing bezel, high water resistance, strong luminous markers for use in low light.
  - **Field watch** — rugged and simple, directly descended from military-issue watches; legibility over everything.
  - **Chronograph** — adds a built-in stopwatch, with sub-dials and pushers to start, stop, and reset.
- **Skeleton watch** — case and dial are cut away to expose the movement itself. The engineering _is_ the design.

## Slide 9: Making the Dark Readable — Radium, Tritium, and Modern Lume

- **Radium** (discovered by Marie Curie in 1898) was mixed into dial paint starting in the early 20th century — it glowed constantly, no "charging" needed.
- The human cost: the **"Radium Girls,"** women employed to hand-paint dials, were taught to point their brushes with their lips, ingesting radioactive paint. Their resulting lawsuits in the 1920s became a landmark case for workplace safety law.
- **Tritium** replaced radium starting in the **1960s** — a much lower-energy form of radiation, safer to be near, but still radioactive, with a roughly 12-year half-life, so tritium lume visibly dims over time. Look for "T Swiss T" printed on a dial.
- Since the **1990s**, **Super-LumiNova / LumiNova** — a non-radioactive strontium-aluminate compound — has become the standard. It doesn't decay or emit radiation; instead it "charges" by absorbing ambient light and re-emitting it in the dark.

## Slide 10: The Quartz Crisis

- **December 25, 1969** — Seiko releases the **Astron**, the first commercially available quartz wristwatch, accurate to about 5 seconds a _month_ — roughly 100x better than the best mechanical chronometers of the day, which were doing about 5 seconds a _day_.
- **Why quartz wins on accuracy:** a small quartz crystal, given a tiny voltage, vibrates at an extremely stable fixed frequency (32,768 Hz) — no mainspring torque curve, no escapement friction, no positional error to fight.
- It was also, eventually, far cheaper to mass-produce than a mechanical movement.
- The fallout for Switzerland was brutal: the number of Swiss watch companies fell from roughly **1,600 in 1970 to under 600 by the mid-1980s**, and industry employment dropped from about **90,000 to under 30,000**. Swiss watch exports fell from ~84 million units a year to ~30 million over the same decade.
- The Swiss industry survived by consolidating (into what became the **Swatch Group**) and repositioning the mechanical watch — no longer the most accurate way to tell time — as a **luxury and craft object** instead. That's still largely how the mechanical watch is sold today.

## Slide 11: Today — Hundreds (If Not Thousands) of Microbrands

- The barriers that once required a factory and a distribution network have mostly disappeared: **crowdfunding** (Kickstarter, Indiegogo), overseas contract manufacturers, and direct-to-consumer platforms let a tiny team — sometimes one person — design, fund, and sell a watch straight to enthusiasts.
- The growth curve has been steep: in 2012, just two successful watch projects on Kickstarter raised about $100,000 combined; three years later, 87 projects raised more than **$57 million**.
- Most microbrands are small by design — producing hundreds to a few thousand watches a year rather than tens of thousands — but there are now **hundreds, arguably thousands**, of them, each with its own niche, aesthetic, and direct relationship with its buyers on forums and social media.
- It's the same democratizing pattern you'd recognize from indie software or indie hardware — just applied to a 700-year-old mechanical craft.
