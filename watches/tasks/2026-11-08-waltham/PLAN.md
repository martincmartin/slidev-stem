## 2. Your three points, against the sources

The sources are in section 8.

**1. "World's first industrial mass production of precision timepieces with
fully interchangeable parts."**

- **"First": true in a narrow form.** The Smithsonian says: "The first firm to
  mass-produce watches by machine was the American Watch Company of
  Massachusetts." Others made watch parts by machine before Waltham: Japy in
  France (rough movements, 43,000 pieces in 1780), Leschot at Vacheron
  Constantin in Geneva (from the late 1830s), and the Pitkin brothers in
  Hartford (800 to 900 watches, 1838–45). Waltham was first at a large scale.
- **"Fully interchangeable": not true for the first decades.** In 1876, two
  Swiss visitors reported that only some parts were interchangeable.
  Favre-Perret: "hand work is still needed" to put a watch with new parts
  into order. David: interchangeability "is not absolute" for pivots, and a
  repairer must send the movement's number to the factory. Watkins, the
  translator, adds that Landes suggests the 1930s for full
  interchangeability. The 1911 parts catalog sells roller jewels for one size
  of watch in eight diameters. My reading: the repairer still chose the one
  that fit.
- **Slide-safe wording:** "Made by machine, to a gauge". "Order by number" is
  true in 1876, 1881 and 1911. Leave "first" and "fully" off the slide.

**2. "Prior to 1850 … a fragmented cottage system … hand-filed and
custom-fitted every gear … expensive … slow … difficult to repair."**

- **True in substance.** England made rough movements in Prescot, Lancashire,
  and finished them in Clerkenwell, London, 200 miles away (Davies).
  Switzerland bought rough movements made by machine (Japy, Fontainemelon)
  and finished them by hand. Many parts came from "piece workers who used
  their own cottages as workshops" (Wikipedia). In both countries, a finished
  watch's parts were fitted to that watch by hand.
- **"Expensive":** Waltham's third batch, 900 watches made after the fall of
  1853, sold at $40 each, cased. An imported movement of the same quality cost
  twice as much (Smithsonian).
- **"Difficult to repair":** for a Swiss watch, "there are no regular
  supplies and repair shops need to have a trained repairer" (David, 1876).
- **"Every gear … hand-filed" overstates it for Swiss rough movements**, which
  machines made. "Each part filed to fit its own watch" is safe.

**3. "Steam-powered machine tools, jigs, and micrometer gauges … stamping,
milling, and drilling … a fraction of a thousandth of an inch."**

- **Steam: true.** In 1876, two steam engines, of 25 and 30 horsepower, drove
  the factory (David). Shafts and belts took the power to each machine. The
  photos of the 1870s show them.
- **Gauges: true.** Waltham "made a model watch and a master set of gauges to
  fit it, and every watch part made thereafter was measured against the
  corresponding model part" (Smithsonian). David describes punching presses,
  and drilling through plate gauges.
- **Micrometers: no source found** for the 1850s to 1870s. Say "gauges".
- **Tolerance: one source.** Scientific American, August 1881: "The
  corresponding parts of their watches will come within one ten-thousandth of
  an inch of being identically alike." I read it only as a reprint, and the
  reprint's other numbers look high for 1881 (2,500 workers; David counted 930
  in 1876). Check the original issue before a slide uses the number.
  $\frac{1}{10\,000}$ inch is $2.54\ \mu\text{m}$. A human hair is about 0.003
  inch, $75\ \mu\text{m}$ (unchecked), so the tolerance is about
  $\frac{1}{30}$ of a hair. "Finer than a hair" holds even for a tolerance of
  $\frac{1}{1\,000}$ inch.

**Also in `docs/rough-slides.md`, "Slide 6"**, two claims have no source that
I found. "More consistently accurate": Favre-Perret argued the reverse for
the best watches; the sources support uniform parts and easy repair. "By 1880
… millions of watches a year": Favre-Perret estimated 250,000 a year for all
American factories in 1876. I did not change that doc.

## 5. Ranking, and why

**B, A, G, C, D, E, F.** The ranks are judgments; no audience tested them.

- **B** shows the turn from hand to machine as two pictures, and covers all
  three points with sources from 1876. It costs one new photo and one credit
  line.
- **A** is B without H4: cheaper, with a larger photo, but the hand work is
  words only. It is the fallback if the two pictures look too small at the
  back.
- **G** gives B's pictures more room, but loses the comparison.
- **C** proves interchangeability best, but shows no factory, and its document
  is from 1911.
- **D** is the best local surprise, but its point is in the speech, not on the
  slide. Its line, "six miles up the Charles from here", works without it.
- **E** is memorable, but the number is not checked, and the drawing costs a
  day.
- **F** is the most work, and still does not show interchangeability.

**Not recommended as additions to B:** a fourth click for the tolerance (it
adds about 8 seconds and a number not yet checked), and the catalog as a third
picture (three pictures in 60 seconds is too many).

## 8. The facts, and their sources

- [Smithsonian, "American Watch Company Prototype"](https://www.si.edu/object/american-watch-company-prototype:nmah_1204764)
  (read on 2026-11-08): "the first firm to mass-produce watches by machine";
  no more than about 2,000 watches made in the United States before the 1850s;
  Dennison's visit to the Springfield Armory; the model watch and its master
  gauges; 17 watches by late 1852, "made mostly by hand"; the third batch of
  900 at $40, half the price of an imported movement.
- [Favre-Perret, report on the Philadelphia Exhibition, 1877](https://watkinsr.id.au/favre.html),
  translated by Richard Watkins, 2004 (© Watkins, so not copied here). The
  Swiss juror. Waltham's display: "2200 gold and silver watches and movements,
  the product six working days at 10 hours per day"; a claimed 425 a day;
  about 250,000 watches a year from all American factories; tests in
  Switzerland found interchangeability "not at all so complete"; his advice to
  the Swiss: "the prompt introduction to Switzerland of the most sophisticated
  machines". He also credits Leschot at Vacheron Constantin with 40 years of
  machine work, and Ingold as "the promoter of the complete manufacture of
  watches by machinery".
- [Jacques David, reports on American watchmaking, 1876](https://watkinsr.id.au/david.html),
  translated by Richard Watkins (© Watkins). Waltham: 930 employees, of whom
  310 were women earning about $1.00 a day and 365 workmen about $2.50; two
  steam engines, 25 and 30 horsepower; a cost of $6 to $9 a movement;
  interchangeability "not absolute" for pivots; spare parts by the movement's
  number. Watkins's notes: some parts were interchangeable and the critical
  ones were not; Landes (_Revolution in Time_) suggests that full
  interchangeability came only in the 1930s.
- [Scientific American, August 1881, as reprinted](https://antique-pocket-watch.com/waltham-pocket-watch-company.html):
  parts alike "within one ten-thousandth of an inch"; a jewel hole two
  ten-thousandths of an inch larger than its pivot; orders "sent by postal
  card, and filled by return mail". Check the original issue.
- [Waltham Watch Company, _Catalogue of Waltham Watch Material_, 1911](https://archive.org/details/catalogueofwalth00walt):
  "Order by Number, stating Size and Model".
- [Wikipedia, "American system of watch manufacturing"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_system_of_watch_manufacturing):
  the Pitkin brothers, Hartford, 1838–45; before the American system, some
  parts came from "piece workers who used their own cottages as workshops".
- [Davies, _The Rise and Decline of England's Watchmaking Industry_](https://www.routledge.com/The-Rise-and-Decline-of-Englands-Watchmaking-Industry-1550-1930/Davies/p/book/9781032131351)
  (the publisher's description): rough movements from Prescot to Clerkenwell,
  200 miles.
- **Six miles:** a straight line from the factory, on Crescent Street in
  Waltham, to Harvard Square or Davis Square is about 9 km, from approximate
  coordinates. Check it on a map, from the venue.
- **H4's four years:** 1755–59, as on the Same Time, Every Tick slide.

### The evidence for quality

- **Waltham's best, 1876: about 5 seconds a day.** The Philadelphia jury
  tested seven first-quality Waltham watches, at $300 each, for 5 days in 5
  positions. Their average daily rates were 5.4, 5.4, 3.8, 4.0, 13.6, 18 and
  8.3 seconds. Without the two that David calls "insufficiently adjusted",
  the average is 5.3 seconds (David, 1876). Watson, an astronomer on the
  jury, then tested them for 15 days. His results put them above the
  chronometers that the Neuchâtel Observatory tested in 1875. David doubts
  this, and Favre-Perret says that Watson compared simple American watches
  with complicated Swiss ones.
- **Reliable:** "stoppages … even defenders of the Swiss watch acknowledge are
  met with more frequently than in American movements" (David, 1876).
- **Uniform:** "This uniformity, which we can say is absolute in the most
  common 18 size" (David, on the movements' sizes). Favre-Perret names
  "a greater uniformity" as the second advantage of machines.
- **As good, for half the price, in the 1850s:** "An imported movement of the
  same quality cost twice as much" (Smithsonian).
- **Cheap ones were ordinary.** David's 12-day test of a cheap William Ellery
  movement (43 francs) gave a rate of 3.45 seconds a day, but its rate varied
  by 11 seconds a day. He found the cheap American movements no better than
  Swiss ones at the same price. So the claim on the slide is for Waltham's
  best, not every Waltham.
- **Not used:** Wikipedia says that Waltham won "the first gold medal in a
  watch precision contest" in 1876, with no source. Favre-Perret says that
  the exhibition gave one kind of award to every exhibitor, a bronze medal
  with a report. Collector sites say that the Pennsylvania Railroad ordered
  Waltham watches in 1866; I found no primary source.
