What Every Nerd Should Know About Mechanical Watches

Martin C. Martin

The figures are the talk's slides, live.

Mechanical watches have kept time for 500 years. This page explains how a modern one works. I personally think it’s fascinating that a purely mechanical device can keep time with 99.99% accuracy for $100. They also changed the world and look good on your wrist.

This presentation was partially inspired by Bartosz Ciechanowski’s Mechanical Watch page. You can think of this talk as a simpler introduction or background. When you’re finished with this, that page gives more depth.

PART 1 Mechanical Marvels

Hairspring

c. 1675

Start with the part that keeps time. In 1675 Christiaan Huygens, a Dutch scientist, invented the hairspring. It is a flat spiral of steel ribbon, finer than a hair. Its outer end is held still, and its inner end is free to turn. Twist the inner end and let go, and the spiral winds and unwinds, back and forth, at a steady rate. In this drawing it swings once every two seconds, and the rest of the watch follows that beat.

Why does a spring swing? Think of a diving board. Bend it down, and it pushes back: an elastic restoring force. Let go, and it does not stop at straight. It overshoots, bends the other way, and comes back again: it oscillates. With no friction, it would oscillate for ever.

A hairspring is a diving board rolled up. The board’s rest shape is a straight line; the hairspring’s is a spiral. Take hold of the slider under the next picture, and drag it to the right.

“Bend” becomes “wind”, and “straight” becomes “rest”. Now give the hairspring a twist: drag the slider to wind the spiral away from its rest shape, and let go.

It pushes back. It overshoots and swings, just as the board did. This one move is the whole idea of the hairspring.

Balance Wheel

c. 1364

The hairspring is only half of the timekeeper. The other half is the balance wheel. While most of its mass is around the outside, it is actually fixed to the spring’s inner end. The spring provides the restoring force. The wheel provides the mass and inertia.

A hairspring alone would oscillate far too fast to count. The wheel slows it to a pace that the gears can count.

Why a wheel, and not a disc?

Mass at the rim counts most, like a figure skater who spins slower with her arms out. Old balance wheels had screws round the rim, and watchmakers moved them in or out to make the watch run slower or faster. The swing also takes about the same time whether it swings through a big angle or small angle (isochronism). That comes from the spring and the mass together, and it is why a watch keeps time even while the watch winds down.

Mass also makes the swing steady. Push an empty playground swing, and it jerks about. Put a child on it and it swings much more gracefully. A heavy wheel carries its own momentum, so a knock to your wrist, or turning it upside down, barely changes its rhythm.

How good is it? A well-calibrated mechanical watch, even an everyday Seiko or Citizen, is about 99.99% accurate. A day has 86,400 seconds, so a good watch gains or loses about nine seconds a day.

I’m not particularly handy with mechanical things, if I tried to build a table it would wobble because things would be off by a few percent. So it kind of blows my mind that, for $100, I can get a simple, everyday, purely mechanical system and tweak it to be 99.99% accurate!

Escapement

c. 1275

A second hand that just moves back and forth isn’t very useful, we need it to turn one way only. The escapement turns the swing into steps.

The escape wheel is being turned by a mechanism we’ll explain soon, so it’s hidden on this slide. Without something to stop it, it would just turn and turn.

The pallet fork stops it.

Its two prongs (called stones) stick in the wheel’s teeth: a tooth lands on a stone, and the wheel stops. Each time the fork rocks, one stone lets a tooth go, and the other stone catches the next tooth. The tooth escapes, and that is where the name comes from.

What rocks the fork? The balance does. On its staff is a small impulse jewel, which sits in the notch at the end of the fork. It’s actually a synthetic ruby or sapphire, so it really is a jewel, a precious stone.

As the balance swings through the middle, the jewel strikes the fork and flips it one way. On the swing back, it flips it the other way.

So, at each swing, the balance lets the wheel take one small step. The jewel hits the fork hard enough to make a sound: this is the tick and the tock of a watch. The push goes both ways, too. At each strike, a little energy flows from the wheel, through the fork, into the balance, and keeps it swinging.

Ticks and Turns

Before we go on, a quick puzzle that trips people up. How long does the second hand take to go once round the dial?

Each step of the escapement moves the second hand one step: one tick, one second. Sixty ticks bring it all the way round, and sixty seconds is a minute. So the second hand makes a complete circle once a minute. A hand is named for what it counts, not for how long it takes make a full 360° turn.

The other hands follow the same rule:

Hand A full turn
Second hand Once a minute
Minute hand Once an hour
Hour hand Twice a day

The minute hand counts sixty minutes, so it turns once an hour. The hour hand counts the twelve hours on the dial, so it turns once in twelve hours: twice a day.

Why is this second hand not in the middle?

On most watches the second hand is in the middle, with the minute and hour hands. But putting three hands all on the same shaft makes the diagram confusing, and many older watches had their second hand off centre. Watchmakers call it small seconds.

Gears

Second hand: c. 1670

The escapement gets the escape wheel turning at a precise rate. But the second hand must turn once a minute, and the escape wheel turns faster than that. To convert a faster rotation into a slower one, a watch uses gears: a small gear that drives a big one.

Here the small gear (in a watch, it is a pinion) has eight teeth, and the big one has sixteen. Four teeth on each gear are coloured. Those four teeth are half of the small gear, but only a quarter of the big one.

The teeth must be the same size, or they would not mesh. Each tooth fits in a gap of the other gear, so the teeth go past each other one for one, and both rims move at the same speed. So the small gear turns twice for each turn of the big one: twice the circumference, half the speed. And exactly so, for ever: unlike a belt, teeth cannot slip.

Must the teeth really be the same size?

Strictly, the pitch must match: a tooth and its gap on one wheel must be as long as a tooth and its gap on the other. One wheel can have wider teeth and narrower gaps.

Now put the escapement back. The gears do not spin freely any more: they move on one tick at a time, with the escape wheel. The escapement controls the whole train of gears.

In this watch, the big gear turns exactly once a minute: just what the second hand needs. So the second hand goes on the big gear’s arbor. It is the first part of the watch that we can read.

Why does this second hand jump, when mine sweeps?

This watch ticks once a second. Most real mechanical watches tick five to eight times a second, so their second hands seem to sweep.

Gear Train

Minute hand: c. 1656

The second hand turns once a minute. The minute hand must turn once an hour: sixty times slower. How do you slow a turn down sixty times?

One gear could do it, with sixty times the teeth of the small gear: 240 teeth. Here it is, with the watch’s case for size. It is far bigger than the watch.

So the watch slows the turn in two stages. The third wheel has 30 teeth, and it drives a pinion of 4 on the second hand’s arbor: seven and a half times slower.

The centre wheel has 32 teeth, and it drives a pinion of 4 on the third wheel’s arbor: eight times slower. Seven and a half times eight is sixty, so the centre wheel turns once an hour.

The minute hand goes on the centre wheel’s arbor, in the middle of the dial. It makes a full turn once an hour.

But what pushes the centre wheel round? The push comes from further back: from the part that powers the whole watch.

Mainspring

c. 1430

The power comes from a spring too: the mainspring, a long ribbon of steel coiled round an arbor. It is not like the hairspring. It does not swing back and forth. It unwinds once, slowly, over a day or two.

Hold the outer end still (the small block is a stud), and turn the arbor with a key. The spring keeps its length, so its coils draw in toward the arbor. Wind the spring away from its rest shape, the dashed line, and it pushes back, as the hairspring does.

Drag the slider slowly to the right: the key winds the spring, three turns. Keep going, and the stud lets go. The outer end swings round until the spring lies on its rest shape again. That fling is the energy that the key put in.

In a watch, the mainspring lives in a drum, the barrel. Its outer end hooks to the barrel’s wall, and its inner end to the arbor. The barrel has teeth, and they drive the train that we built. Wind the arbor, and the watch starts: the balance and the escapement let the train turn one tick at a time.

Now the arbor is held, so the spring can let down only by turning the barrel. Step the figure forward, and six hours pass in six seconds. The barrel turns once, the minute hand six times, and the coils move back out to the wall. When the spring is let down, the watch stops.

Hour Hand

Hour hand: c. 1350

The minute hand turns once an hour. The hour hand must turn twice a day: once in twelve hours, twelve times slower.

Twelve to one is not much more than the eight to one of the last stage. One pinion on the centre arbor, and a wheel with twelve times its teeth, would do it.

But put the hour hand on that wheel’s arbor, and it is off centre! An hour hand must be in the middle, under the minute hand. And it would run backward: a gear turns the other way from the gear that drives it.

So the watch takes two steps instead, close to the middle of the watch.

The cannon pinion, on the centre arbor, drives the minute wheel: three to one, out from the centre.

The minute wheel’s own pinion drives the hour wheel, which turns on the centre arbor: four to one, back to the middle. Three times four is twelve, and the second step turns the hour wheel the right way round again.

The hour hand goes on the hour wheel, in the middle, under the minute hand. It turns twice a day.
