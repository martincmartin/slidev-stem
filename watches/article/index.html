What Everyone Should Know About Mechanical Watches

Martin C. Martin

The figures are the talk's slides, live.

Mechanical watches have kept time for 500 years. Many of their core parts were invented even earlier for giant cathedral tower clocks before being shrunk into portable timepieces. This page explains how a modern one works. I personally think it’s fascinating that a purely mechanical device can keep time with 99.99% accuracy for $100. They also changed the world and look good on your wrist.

This presentation was partially inspired by Bartosz Ciechanowski’s Mechanical Watch page. You can think of this page as a simpler introduction or background. When you’re finished with this, that page gives more depth.

PART 1 Mechanical Marvels

Hairspring

c. 1675

Start with the part that keeps time. In 1675 Christiaan Huygens, a Dutch scientist, invented the hairspring. It is a flat spiral of steel ribbon, finer than a hair. Its outer end is held still, and its inner end is free to turn. Twist the inner end and let go, and the spiral winds and unwinds, back and forth, at a steady rate. In this drawing it swings once every two seconds, and the rest of the watch follows that beat.

Why does a spring swing? Think of a diving board. Bend it down, and it pushes back: an elastic restoring force. Let go, and it does not stop at straight. It overshoots, bends the other way, and comes back again: it oscillates. With no friction, it would oscillate forever.

A hairspring is a diving board rolled up. The board’s rest shape is a straight line; the hairspring’s is a spiral. Take hold of the slider under the next picture, and drag it to the right.

“Bend” becomes “wind”, and “straight” becomes “rest”. Now give the hairspring a twist: drag the slider to wind the spiral away from its rest shape, and let go.

It pushes back. It overshoots and swings, just as the board did. This one move is the whole idea of the hairspring.

Balance Wheel

c. 1364

The hairspring is only half of the timekeeper. The other half is the balance wheel. While most of its mass is around the outside, it is actually fixed to the spring’s inner end. The spring provides the restoring force. The wheel provides the mass and inertia.

A hairspring alone would oscillate far too fast to count. The wheel slows it to a pace that the gears can count.

Why a wheel, and not a disc?

Mass at the rim counts most, like a figure skater who spins slower with her arms out. Old balance wheels had screws around the rim, and watchmakers moved them in or out to make the watch run slower or faster. The swing also takes about the same time whether it swings through a big angle or small angle (isochronism). That comes from the spring and the mass together, and it is why a watch keeps time even while the watch winds down.

Mass also makes the swing steady. Push an empty playground swing, and it jerks about. Put a child on it and it swings much more gracefully. A heavy wheel carries its own momentum, so a knock to your wrist, or turning it upside down, barely changes its rhythm.

How good is it? A well-calibrated mechanical watch, even an everyday Seiko or Citizen, is about 99.99% accurate. A day has 86,400 seconds, so that watch gains or loses about nine seconds a day.

I’m not particularly handy with mechanical things. If I tried to build a table it would wobble because things would be off by a few percent. So it kind of blows my mind that, for $100, I can get a simple, everyday, purely mechanical system and tweak it to be 99.99% accurate!

Escapement

c. 1275

A second hand that just moves back and forth isn’t very useful. We need it to turn one way only. The escapement turns the swing into steps.

The escape wheel is being turned by a mechanism we’ll explain soon, so it’s hidden on this diagram. Without something to stop it, it would just turn and turn.

The pallet fork stops it.

Its two prongs (called stones) stick in the wheel’s teeth: a tooth lands on a stone, and the wheel stops. Each time the fork rocks, one stone lets a tooth go, and the other stone catches the next tooth. The tooth escapes, and that is where the name comes from.

What rocks the fork? The balance does. On its staff is a small impulse jewel, which sits in the notch at the end of the fork. It’s actually a synthetic ruby or sapphire, so it really is a jewel, a precious stone.

As the balance swings through the middle, the jewel strikes the fork and flips it one way. On the swing back, it flips it the other way.

So, at each swing, the balance lets the wheel take one small step. The step ends when the next tooth lands on a stone, hard enough to make a sound, this is the tick and the tock of a watch.

Ticks and Turns

Before we go on, something that trips people up until they think it through. How long does the second hand take to go once around the dial?

Each tick is one second, and sixty ticks brings it all the way around. So the second hand makes a complete circle once a minute. A hand is named for what it counts, not for how long it takes to make a full 360° turn.

Hand A full turn
Second hand Once a minute
Minute hand Once an hour
Hour hand Twice a day

Why is this second hand not in the middle?

On most watches the second hand is in the middle, with the minute and hour hands. But putting three hands all on the same shaft makes the diagram confusing, and many older watches had their second hand off center. Watchmakers call it small seconds.

Gears

Second hand: c. 1670

The escapement gets the escape wheel turning at a precise rate. But the second hand must turn once a minute, and the escape wheel turns faster than that. To convert a faster rotation into a slower one, a watch uses gears: a small gear that meshes with a big one.

Must the teeth be the same size?

Strictly, the pitch must match: a tooth and its gap on one wheel must be as long as a tooth and its gap on the other. One wheel can have wider teeth than the other, as long as it has narrower gaps.

The teeth on the two gears must have the same spacing, so they mesh neatly. Each tooth fits in a gap of the other gear, so the teeth go past each other one for one, and both rims move at the same speed. Four teeth on each gear are colored. Those four teeth are half of the small gear, but only a quarter of the big one. So the small gear turns twice for each turn of the big one: twice the circumference, half the speed.

Why does this second hand jump, when mine sweeps?

This watch ticks once a second, to make the hairspring slow enough to see. Most real mechanical watches tick five to eight times a second, so their second hands seem to sweep.

Now attach the smaller gear (called a pinion) to the escape wheel. The gears do not spin freely any more: they move on one tick at a time, with the escape wheel. The escapement controls the speed of the whole train of gears.

The big gear pushing the small gear (pinion) is the force turning the escape wheel. What turns the big wheel? That’s a mechanism we’ll explain soon.

In this watch, the big gear (called the fourth wheel) turns exactly once a minute: just what the second hand needs! So the second hand goes on the big gear’s axle. It is the first part of the watch that we can read.

Gear Train

Minute hand: c. 1656

The second hand turns once a minute. The minute hand must turn once an hour: sixty times slower. So we could try the same trick, and have a gear that’s 60 times bigger than the small gear.

But there’s a limit to how small you can manufacture the small gear, so the big gear would be huge. And it would have a lot of teeth which adds to the cost. Plus, it would turn the wrong way, counterclockwise, because every gear turns the opposite direction from the one it meshes with.

So the watch slows in two stages. The third wheel runs 7½ times slower, and the center wheel runs 8 times slower than that.

7½ times 8 is 60, so the center wheel turns once an hour.

The minute hand goes on the center wheel’s axle (arbor), in the middle of the dial. It makes a full turn once an hour.

The center wheel pushes the third wheel which pushes the fourth wheel which pushes the escape wheel. Next up, the mechanism that pushes the center wheel and powers the whole watch.

Mainspring

c. 1430

This is a spiral spring, like the hairspring. But instead of swinging back and forth, it does one slow unwind over a day or two. In the illustration above, it’s at rest.

Below, the outer end is held in place. Drag the slider to wind it up. The spring keeps its length, so its coils draw in toward the center.

When wound away from its rest shape, the dashed line, it pushes back, as the hairspring does. A tiny one-way ratchet locks the axle (arbor) so it can only turn one way. To unwind the mainspring, let go of the outer end by pressing the button.

The block is gone, and the outer end swings around until the spring lies on its rest shape again. That fling is the energy that the winding put in.

A watch harnesses that force by attaching the spring’s outer end to a toothed drum called the barrel, invented by Jean-Antoine Lépine around 1760.

As the mainspring pushes the barrel, the barrel’s teeth drive the gear train we just built. This is the “hidden mechanism” we mentioned earlier.

Because the ratchet stops the arbor from turning backwards, the spring has only one way to unwind: by its outer end turning the barrel. Step the figure forward using the controls under it, and six hours pass in six seconds. The barrel turns once, the minute hand six times, and the coils move back out to the wall. When the spring is completely unwound, the watch stops.

Hour Hand

Hour hand: c. 1350

The hour hand must turn twice a day. That’s once every twelve hours. Unlike the 60:1, a 12:1 gear is doable.

But the hour hand on that wheel’s axle (arbor) is off center! And it would run backward (counterclockwise): a pair of meshing gears turn opposite directions.

So we use the same two-step trick we used for the minute hand. This time, 3:1 going out and 4:1 coming back. 3:1 × 4:1 = 12:1.

The hour hand goes on the hour wheel, in the middle, under the minute hand, attached to a hollow tube (pipe) that slips over the minute hand’s axle. It turns clockwise, twice a day.

PART 2 Changed The World

Age of Sail: Longitude Was a Guess

In April 1741, George Anson’s squadron rounded Cape Horn and turned north into the Pacific.

Their estimated position put them well out to sea, west of the land.

At about 2 a.m., a lookout saw cliffs two miles ahead. The ships were heading straight toward them.

Their latitude told them how far north or south they were. They could check it from the height of the Sun at noon.

But longitude was their position east or west. Their estimate put the land far beyond the horizon.

Their longitude estimate was about 400 miles off. They had no reliable way to measure it at sea.

Hunt for Juan Fernández Island, 1741

Anson’s men were dying of scurvy: four to six a day. They needed fresh water and food from the island of Juan Fernández, off Chile.

On May 28, they reached the island’s latitude. But had they sailed too far east, or too far west?

They chose east. Two days later, they saw the coast of Chile. The island was behind them, and the coast belonged to their enemy.

They turned west again. Headwinds and calms stretched the return journey to nine days: the same distance that had taken two days eastward.

At dawn on June 9, they finally saw the island. They still needed another day and night to reach its anchorage.

The detour had cost 70 to 80 men. They had been only a few hours from the island when they turned the wrong way.

What the count means

The figure uses six deaths a day to show the cost of time. Its final count is 72. These daily numbers are illustrative, not a recorded list of deaths. Walter’s account, Book I, chapter X, reports four to six deaths a day and a total of 70 to 80.

Admiral Shovell Sails Home, 1707

Thirty-four years earlier, Admiral Shovell’s fleet was sailing home from the Mediterranean. Twenty-one ships approached the English Channel in poor weather.

On October 22, 1707, the fleet’s estimated position lay south and west of the Isles of Scilly. The ships sailed on.

At about 8 p.m., in the dark, the flagship struck the rocks of Scilly.

The fleet was farther north and east than its navigators thought. The gap between the ships’ estimated and actual positions was now a line to the rocks.

Four warships sank. More than 1,400 men died.

A disputed cause

The Royal Museums Greenwich account discusses errors in navigation, compasses, and weather. The cause remains disputed. This map shows the position error; it does not establish one cause for the wreck. The museum also finds no firm evidence that this disaster caused the Longitude Act. Accounts of the disaster give death tolls from 1,400 to more than 2,000.

The Longitude Prize, 1714

Seven years after Scilly, Parliament passed the Longitude Act. Disasters like these fed the push for it. The Act offered a fortune to anyone who could find a ship’s longitude at sea.

Within one degree, about 70 miles, the prize was £10,000: about 2.3 million dollars today.

Within half a degree, about 35 miles, it was £20,000: about 4.6 million dollars today. Half the error earned twice the money. At Cape Noir, Anson’s squadron had been 400 miles off.

Which degree?

The Act measures the error in degrees of a great circle. One such degree is 60 nautical miles, about 69 miles, at any latitude. A degree of longitude is that long only at the equator. At Boston’s latitude, it is about 51 miles. The Act also offered £15,000 for two-thirds of a degree.

Longitude From Time

Why would a clock help? Because noon does not come to everyone at once. The Earth turns toward the east, so the Sun’s highest point sweeps west. When it is noon in Greenwich, the part of London that gave Greenwich Mean Time its name, a ship 30 degrees to the west has not reached noon yet.

In an hour, the Earth turns by 15 degrees, one line on this globe. Noon has moved 15 degrees west, and it has not reached the ship yet.

A navigator can measure noon at sea: it is when the Sun stands highest. After a second hour, noon reaches the ship. Suppose that the ship carries a clock that still keeps Greenwich’s time. At the ship’s noon, it reads 2:00. Two hours behind Greenwich means 30 degrees west of it.

So the clock measures the ship’s longitude. Take the hours behind Greenwich, multiply by 15, and that is how many degrees west the ship is.

The Sun in the picture

The real Sun is behind you as you look at this globe, and over the Equator. The picture puts it under the globe so that it fits, and leaves the night where the real Sun would put it. The real noon also runs up to about 16 minutes ahead of or behind a steady clock through the year. Navigators corrected for that with a table, the equation of time.

How Good a Watch Wins the Prize?

How good must that clock be? Sixty minutes for 15 degrees is 4 minutes a degree. So to find the longitude within 1 degree, the clock must be right within 4 minutes. To find it within half a degree, the Act’s top prize, the clock must be right within 2 minutes.

Now take a voyage of 6 weeks, or 42 days. To stay within 4 minutes, the clock can gain or lose only about 6 seconds a day. To stay within 2 minutes, it can gain or lose only about 3. An everyday mechanical watch today gains or loses about 9 seconds a day, on dry land. The prize asked for 3, on a rolling ship.

A steady error is not the problem

A clock that gains exactly 6 seconds every day is as good as a perfect one, if the navigator knows it: subtract 6 seconds a day. What the prize really needs is a clock whose rate does not change.

Isaac Newton, 1714

Could anyone build such a clock? In 1714, a committee on the question asked the most famous scientist alive: Isaac Newton, of the laws of motion and gravity. A watch that keeps time exactly, he said, was one way to find longitude. But, in the words of the committee’s record:

A ship rolls and pitches. The weather swings from the tropics to the cold. Even gravity is not the same everywhere, and a clock can feel it. Newton’s verdict was not that it was impossible, only that no one had done it.

Whose handwriting?

The Royal Museums Greenwich holds the record of his words, in a copy from about 1741. The script on the slide is a modern font in the spirit of that copy’s hand. It is not Newton’s own hand. He also named two other ways to find longitude: the eclipses of Jupiter’s moons, and the place of the Moon. Neither, he said, could be used at sea yet.

John Harrison: A Carpenter's Side Project

Newton said that such a watch had not yet been made. That same year, John Harrison turned 21, in the north of England. He was a carpenter by trade, and he taught himself to make clocks.

His aim was a clock for the sea that kept time within 3 seconds a day. In 1730, at about 37, he began one at home in Lincolnshire, probably with help from his brother James. It took 5 years. H1 has two linked balances that a ship’s motion does not upset, and runs with no oil at all. To see it move, watch this 30-second clip of H1 at Greenwich.

What is in the painting?

Behind him is H3, in its traveling case. The watch in his hand is not H4: it is a watch of 1753, made to his design by John Jefferys. That watch showed him that a small timekeeper could work.

Same Time, Every Tick

Four clocks, one problem. Each tick is one swing of the balance, and every swing must take the same time, on a rolling ship, hot or cold. H1 sailed to Lisbon and back in 1736. It did poorly on the way out. On the way home, the ship’s master put the ship 60 miles east of where it was, and H1 was right. That won him money for a better clock.

H2 took 2 years. He found a fault in its balances: a ship’s turn changes the time of their swing. So he abandoned it for H3. It never sailed.

H3 took 19 years, and it never sailed. The swings of its two large, heavy balances still varied.

But look at what he made on the way. To cancel the effect of heat, he joined brass and steel in a bimetallic strip, which still works in thermostats. To cut friction, he kept rollers apart in a caged roller bearing, which is now nearly everywhere in machinery.

Then he gave up on big clocks. H4 took 4 years and weighs about 3 pounds: from the outside, a large pocket watch. Its balance is small and fast, with five ticks a second. In 1761, it went on a sea trial.

Did H4 Win the Prize?

Did it win? To win the top prize, the Act asked for a clock that was right within 2 minutes after 6 weeks at sea.

H4 sailed for Jamaica in November 1761. After 81 days, it was 5 seconds slow.

The Board was not convinced, and sent it on a second trial, to Barbados, where it kept time within 39 seconds. In 1765, the Board agreed that it had kept time within the strictest limit of the Act. But the money came slowly: 16 payments over 36 years, £23,065 in all, most of it at the end. The last two payments are more than 70% of the total. The last, £8,750, came in 1773, 43 years after he started. He had appealed to King George III. “By God, Harrison, I will see you righted!” the King told his son William, and Parliament acted. He was 80. He died three years later. The Act’s official prize was never given to anyone.
